## ADDBC85245F6

At the entrance of a hall at the India International Centre stand two painted wooden columns. A gap above each shows plainly that neither touches the roof. One visitor called them a deception, because they carry nothing. Another said they make the entrance look ceremonial. I have not entered the hall, nor spoken to the designer, nor read a notice giving the reason.

This is a useful little case. A thing may be judged by its load, and then it is useless; or by its effect, and then it is a sign. The columns do not support the building, but they support the manner of entering: they frame the threshold and make the step into the hall a visible act. The first judgment is mechanical; the second is aesthetic. Both are true, and both are incomplete.

Architecture often speaks before it is asked. A painted column asks the passer-by to treat the entrance as more than a hole in a wall. Whether this is deception or ceremony depends on what one expects architecture to do: only to bear weight, or also to give form to custom. Without the designer's intention, the columns remain an appearance that has become a question.

## C2A3366C420C

At the entrance to a hall stand two painted wooden columns. Above each there is a gap; neither touches the roof. The arrangement is not hidden. One visitor called it a deception, because the columns carry nothing. Another said they make the entrance look ceremonial. Both remarks rest on what is visible. The first asks whether the column is a member in the building’s structure; the second asks whether it is a sign in the building’s appearance.

The question is not settled by saying that one is right and the other wrong. A column that bears no weight may still perform a function: it frames, announces, and makes the entrance intelligible as an entrance. Yet if the gap is seen, the claim of structural support is no longer available. What remains is a representation, not a load. Whether this is deception or ceremony depends on the intention of the maker, which is unknown here. I have not entered the hall, and no notice states the purpose. It is possible that the columns were meant to recall an architecture where such members are ornamental, or to give a plain doorway a more formal character. It is also possible that the gap is accidental, or that the columns were added later for convenience. Until the designer’s purpose is known, the columns remain a visible tension: they suggest support while openly refusing it, and they turn the threshold into a question about what a building is allowed to promise.

## 54A239A4177E

My dear Karl,

I write from Delhi on 16 October, though I have no recent letter from you, and I do not know what circumstances attend you there. I will not fill this sheet with guesses.

Today at a small outdoor lesson I watched a boy set a calculation on his slate. The teacher crossed out the result without giving a reason. The boy erased it, copied the teacher’s number, and asked whether he could now leave. I did not see the original calculation clearly enough to judge it. Perhaps it was wrong; perhaps it was right. The crossing-out had no visible ground beyond the teacher’s hand.

That is what struck me: a mistake, if there was one, was not corrected by explanation, but by authority. The boy learned the number, not the movement by which it came. One can be wrong and know why; one can also be made to forget that one was wrong, and so lose the power to find one’s way again.

I hope you are well, and that your own slate is not always cleaned by another’s hand.

Your father, G. Hegel
