### wallace-logic:371

Plain:
The connection between the external and the internal combines the two previous relationships while simultaneously suspending simple relativity and general phenomenality. However, as long as the understanding holds the internal and external as strictly separate, they remain empty forms, with each being just as void as the other. In examining both the natural world and the spiritual realm, it is crucial to correctly assess this internal-external dynamic, specifically by rejecting the error that views the internal as the sole essential core while dismissing the external as insignificant. This error appears when people frequently reduce the distinction between nature and mind to the abstract gap between the inner and the outer. Regarding nature, it certainly exists in the crude external, not just relative to the mind, but intrinsically. Yet, describing it as external "in the gross" does not suggest an abstract externality, since no such thing exists. Instead, it indicates that the Idea, which serves as the shared content of nature and mind, appears in nature solely as external, and consequently, solely as internal. The abstract understanding, relying on its "either-or" logic, might resist this view of nature. Nevertheless, this view is clearly evident in our other forms of awareness, especially within religion.

Original:
The relation of Outward and Inward unites the two relations that precede, and at the same time sets in abeyance mere relativity and phenomenality in general. Yet so long as understanding keeps the Inward and Outward fixed in their separation, they are empty forms, the one as null as the other. Not only in the study of nature, but also of the spiritual world, much depends on a just appreciation of the relation of inward and outward, and especially on avoiding the misconception that the former only is the essential point on which everything turns, while the latter is unessential and trivial. We find this mistake made when, as is often done, the difference between nature and mind is traced back to the abstract difference between inner and outer. As for nature, it certainly is in the gross external, not merely to the mind, but even on its own part. But to call it external 'in the gross' is not to imply an abstract externality--for there is no such thing. It means rather that the Idea which forms the common content of nature and mind, is found in nature as outward only, and for that very reason only inward. The abstract understanding, with its 'Either--or,' may struggle against this conception of nature. It is none the less obviously found in our other modes of consciousness, particularly in religion.
