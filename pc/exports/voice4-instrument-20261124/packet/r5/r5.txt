My journey did not take me through Swabia, but via Nuremberg to Berlin. It is a strange feeling to be here in Vienna, where one is viewed with a certain distrust as a Berliner. I often think of our sons Karl and Immanuel, and of Ludwig. In the library, surrounded by the silence of art, I feel the distance from our home.

The art here is great, but it is also a mirror of society. As in the history of freedom, so also in aesthetics: The individual must be sublated in the universal to become true. Eberle’s dance was good, but Torelli’s posture carried the weight of antiquity within it. It is a lesson that also applies to us: We must submit to discipline in order to attain freedom.

I hope that Karl and Immanuel are getting along well at school and show the necessary calm and attention. The school must mediate between the love of the family and the world of action. I expect that they learn to follow before they judge.

Stay healthy, my dear Marie. I am drinking a good wine here and thinking of you.

Your
G. W. F. Hegel

Vienna, September 21, 1824

My dear Marie,

I am sitting down to tell you about my stay in Vienna today, after I have returned home from the opera. The day was full of impressions, both in spirit and in body, and I hope you will forgive the brevity, as fatigue is now overcoming me.

In the morning, I began with a visit to St. Stephen's Church, where I took a brief overview of the imperial picture painting in the Belvedere. The works of art speak of a certain perfection that unfolds only in the history of freedom, but today I limit myself to mere perception. Afterwards, I hurried to the post office to receive your letter – a precious comfort in the distance – and subsequently I had to settle my passport matters at the customs office, a tedious but necessary duty of the traveler.

In the evening, however, the true pleasure awaited: the Italian Opera. Rubini and Donzelli appeared as tenors and sang a duet that deeply impressed me. The bassist Lablache was also present, although he did not have a leading role. It is interesting to see how German vocal art shows itself here; the singer Eckerlin is currently present, and he is compared with Madame Milder. Madame Fodor is playing in Othello today, which indicates a significant performance. After the opera, there followed a pas de deux by two Frenchmen, which reflected the elegance of the Parisian school.

On the way home, I found Lilli and Klein at my house. They are staying here this week and have spent the last three evenings at the Casperl and German Theatre, without seeing or hearing the Italian Opera. We have agreed to go out together, for their presence enlivens the household. Partheys young wife was unwell in her room, which explains her absence.

I will remain in Vienna as long as the money for the opera and the return journey lasts. It is a precious time that I am using to experience the culture of this metropolis, before I return to the duty of my lectures. I am now going to dinner to still the fatigue.

Your faithful
G. W. F. Hegel

Herzberg, 8 October 1824

Dearest Marie,

The journey so far has been ordinary and bearable, for the weather has turned for the better. On Sunday the sky was overcast, and it rained several times, until we reached Jüterbog. Today, on Monday, I arrived in Herzberg after lunch.

Here I have experienced something unplanned. I asked a rural pastor to arrange a niece as a companion for me to Dresden. The lady was not ugly, but she has grown a little old and possesses a Saxon politeness that did not much encourage me to speak. So the journey was similar to a solitude. A cat-in-the-bag purchase has been made, and the find was not bad, but insignificant.

Immediately upon arrival I went to the inn, where I heard that Privy Councillor Schulze is staying here. I entered the Blue Star and sought him out. He had already prepared everything for a departure today, yet I was able to persuade him to stay with me today. We were both very glad to see each other again.

This morning we first visited Böttiger, then the picture gallery. Afterwards we listened to Böttiger’s lecture, in which we went through the antiquities again. If I were a true English traveller, I would not have declined the company; but I am still deeply German and could not resist the invitation. The encounter was pleasant, and the conversation flowed easily.

I think of you and the boys in Berlin. The silence here is different, but the acquaintance with Schulze makes the days less lonely.

Your faithful
