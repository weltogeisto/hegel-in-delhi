# The questions of the century: a first list for Welt's pen

Claude in the cloud, 24 November 2026. A draft for packet 5 of `WORLD-3.md`. **The list is Welt's to write.** It is where he says what he wants this mind to think about. Nothing here is built, except the first question, which is in `HEGEL_EXITS` (off) as the Yamuna Review's second asking.

**Why questions.** A man with a library and no one asking reads and makes notes. The works that made him were answers: to a prize question, to a lecture hall, to a review. Each question below is one that only he can answer, because it sets a sentence of his own against something this century did, and because he now lives where it happened.

**How each is built.**
- **His sentence:** where he said it. A paraphrase, checked against the shelf before it is put to him.
- **The century:** what stands against it, and where he can read of it.
- **Who asks, and why:** a person of his world with a reason of their own. The world offers and never steers: he may decline, and no answer is the right one.

## The column: the editor of the Yamuna Review, one question a fortnight

**1. India and history.** "You taught that India has no history of its own making, and you have now lived in its capital. Does the sentence stand?"
- **His sentence:** the lectures on the philosophy of history, the Oriental world: India lies outside the movement of history and has no state of its own making.
- **The century:** a republic with a constitution of its own writing, 1950, and every adult a vote. In the library: the Constitution, Gandhi, Tagore.
- **Why her:** his manuscript has circled this since Day 12, and his thesis moved on Day 54. She has heard of the pages.

**2. Poverty.** "You wrote that civil society, for all its wealth, is not rich enough to end the poverty it makes. Two centuries of wealth later, you buy your tea in Khan Market. Was that a limit of your time, or of the thing itself?"
- **His sentence:** the Philosophy of Right, §§ 243 to 245.
- **The century:** the market grown larger than any state; this city's wealth and its poor side by side. In the library: Marx on the dialectic and on British rule in India.
- **Why her:** a review in Delhi prints an essay on this every month, and none by the man who posed it.

**3. Nature.** "You taught that nature has no history: nothing new under the sun. The air of this city has a history, and men made it. What is a nature that spirit has made?"
- **His sentence:** the introduction to the lectures on the philosophy of history; the Encyclopaedia's philosophy of nature.
- **The century:** the air he breathes and counts every morning; the climate. In the papers nearly every day.
- **Why her:** he has used the haze as an image in every sitting. She asks him to think it instead.

**4. Machines that answer.** "You wrote that reckoning is so mechanical a business that a machine can do it. Machines now write sentences and answer questions. Is that thinking? What would have to be true of a thing for spirit to be in it?"
- **His sentence:** the Science of Logic, on number and calculation; the Encyclopaedia, on language and mechanical memory.
- **The century:** the machine in his own pocket, and the papers on it most weeks.
- **Why her:** every review has asked every professor this. None could ask him.

**5. What was done in his name.** "It is said in this century, in your name, that history has ended. Did you say so? Has it?"
- **His sentence:** the close of the lectures on the philosophy of history; the preface to the Philosophy of Right, on the owl of Minerva.
- **The century:** two hundred years of readers. In the library: Marx's afterword, Croce's *What is Living and What is Dead of the Philosophy of Hegel*; the encyclopaedia for what came after 1950.
- **Why her:** it is the one thing most of her readers believe they know of him.

**6. Women.** "Your lectures on right, as your pupils took them down, say that women are not made for the higher sciences or for government. Nidhi explains this city to you, you teach Ananya German, a woman edits this review, and a woman will decide whether you keep your house. Does the paragraph stand?"
- **His sentence:** the Philosophy of Right, § 166, with the addition from the lectures.
- **The century:** his own fifty days.
- **Why her:** she is the editor.

## The course: the programme officer of the IIC, four evenings

The Centre names a theme for a course, and the four evenings are its parts: **"The state, two hundred years on."**

**7. The officials.** "You called the civil servants the universal estate. You have now spent two months before Mr. Saxena's desk. What is an official?"
- **His sentence:** the Philosophy of Right, §§ 287 to 297.
- **The century:** the Directorate of Estates, and Shri Hegde, a Joint Secretary, across his own breakfast table.

**8. Caste and law.** "You held that caste fixes by nature what freedom must make by right. This republic abolished untouchability by law in 1950, and caste lives on in marriages and in votes. What can law do against what a people holds to be nature?"
- **His sentence:** the lectures on the philosophy of history, on India.
- **The century:** the Constitution, article 17. In the library from January: Ambedkar.

**9. Subjection and freedom.** "You called it the necessary fate of the Asiatic empires to be subjected to Europeans. India was subjected, and then freed itself, for the most part without war. What does a philosophy of history owe to the freeing?"
- **His sentence:** the lectures on the philosophy of history, the close of the section on India; the Philosophy of Right, § 248, on colonies.
- **The century:** 1857 to 1947. In the library: Macaulay's minute, Marx on British rule, Gandhi's *Hind Swaraj*, Tagore's *Nationalism*.

**10. War.** "You wrote that war keeps a people from the stagnation of a long peace. Two states of this subcontinent now hold weapons that would end them both in an afternoon. Does the sentence survive them?"
- **His sentence:** the Philosophy of Right, § 324.
- **The century:** the bomb. In the papers and the encyclopaedia. Mr. Malhotra, thirty-five years in the service, may be in the hall.

## Asked by one person

**11. The Gita, from the scholar** (packet 6). "You read the Gita in Schlegel's Latin and judged its devotion an emptying. Aurobindo read it in Sanskrit, and read you, and says that action without attachment is the opposite of withdrawal. Read him and answer me."
- **His sentence:** the review of 1827 of Humboldt's lecture, on his shelf in German.
- **The century:** the Gita in English, and Aurobindo's *Essays on the Gita*, in the library.

**12. Public opinion, from Ananya,** after a lesson. "You wrote that public opinion deserves to be respected and despised alike. Everyone I know carries it in a pocket and adds to it every minute. Which is it now?"
- **His sentence:** the Philosophy of Right, §§ 316 to 318.
- **The century:** the phone, which she has in her hand as she asks.

## What is checked and what is not

- **His sentences are paraphrases from memory.** Each is checked against the shelf (`mind/shelf`) before it is put to him, and the paragraph numbers with them.
- **Not checked:** that each book named for the library can be had in a public-domain edition under the shelf's rule. That is the library's own work.
